01 Background

On November 21, 2023, Binance reached a joint settlement with four U.S. federal agencies covering conduct dating back to 2017. Binance pleaded guilty to conspiracy to operate an unlicensed money transmitting business and to willful failure to maintain an effective anti-money-laundering program. Founder and CEO Changpeng Zhao ("CZ") pleaded guilty personally and stepped down.

Separately, OFAC found that from around August 2017 to October 2022, Binance allowed trades between US persons and users in sanctioned jurisdictions — including Iran, Syria, North Korea, Cuba, Crimea, and the self-declared Donetsk and Luhansk republics.

This analysis treats the case the way a GRC review would: which controls were missing, why, and whether the mandated remediation actually closes the gap.

02 Business Classification

Under FinCEN's 2019 guidance, a centralized exchange facilitating conversion of virtual currency for fiat or other virtual currency is a Money Services Business (MSB) — specifically a money transmitter — and must register with FinCEN and run a BSA-compliant AML program if it serves US customers, regardless of where it's headquartered.

Binance's core failure sat at the governance level, not the product level — unlike a case such as Ethena's MiCA gap, where the product itself was structurally incompatible with the rule. Binance operated globally without ever registering as an MSB, and without a program proportionate to its size or risk.

04 Root Cause Classification

The question that matters most for a GRC read of any enforcement case: was this structural (the business model itself can't comply) or operational (the controls needed to exist but simply didn't)?

Structural failure
Product can't comply
The business model itself conflicts with the rule at the design level — no policy fix closes it, the product needs rebuilding. (This was Ethena's case under MiCA.)
Operational failure — Binance
Controls didn't exist
Nothing about running a crypto exchange is inherently incompatible with BSA/AML. Competitors register as MSBs and screen for sanctions successfully. Growth was prioritized over building a program proportionate to it — including flagged internal risks that were set aside rather than acted on.

Overall: unlike a structural gap, this one is realistically closeable — but it depends on genuine internal culture change, not just paper policy updates, which is exactly why regulators required years of external oversight instead of a one-time attestation.
